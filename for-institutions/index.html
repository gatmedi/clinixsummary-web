The shape of it

Documentation is not one document

A single encounter has to become several artefacts, each written for a different reader. Today they are written separately, at different times, and drift apart. Here they are produced together from one source, so they cannot disagree.

Worked example

A routine diabetes review, end to end

Nothing below is edited for effect. This is what one recording produces, in the order it produces it.

1

What the clinician actually said

41 seconds · no structure, no headings
Recording Verbatim
“Right — Margaret Doyle, sixty-two, back for her three-month diabetes review. She's on metformin one gram twice daily, we added empagliflozin ten milligrams in March. Last HbA1c was sixty-eight, today it's fifty-eight, so that's come down nicely. She's been getting pins and needles in both feet, about two months now, worse at night. No ulcers, pedal pulses present, but monofilament shows reduced sensation both sides to the mid-foot. BP today one forty-two over eighty-eight, she's on ramipril five. Retinal screening was January, no retinopathy. Plan — continue current therapy, start amitriptyline ten at night for the neuropathic symptoms, increase the ramipril to ten, bloods again in three months, refer to podiatry for ongoing foot care, review three months.”

The template is theirs. A department uploads the Word proformas it already uses. The system reads them — including their tables — and writes into that structure. Fields the clinician did not mention are left blank rather than filled with a plausible default, because a blank field is a true record and an invented one is not.

Both letters are written from the finished note, not from the recording. That is deliberate: it is what makes the letter and the record agree. A letter that contradicts the note it came from is worse than no letter at all.

The fourth column is the point. Most denials are not disputes about the care — they are disputes about whether the record supports the code. Because the codes are derived from the note rather than chosen alongside it, every code arrives with the line that justifies it, and a code with nothing behind it does not get suggested.

Automated billing

An inpatient stay, priced from the record

Outpatient coding is one line. An admission is thirty, spread over several days and several people, and it is where money is actually lost — not to fraud, but to items nobody wrote down. The same documentation that produces the notes produces the bill.

The codes are yours, not ours. A hospital uploads the charge catalogue it already bills from — its own item codes, descriptions and tariffs. Documentation is matched against that catalogue, and mapped on to whichever classification the payer requires: ICD-10-AM and ACHI where those are mandated, CPT elsewhere, and the national scheme where one exists — in Saudi Arabia, submission through NPHIES. Nothing is re-keyed into a second system.

Nothing quietly dropped

The second set of bloods, the fifth antibiotic dose, the second ward day. These are not disputed items — they are items nobody got round to entering. Reading them from the record is how they stop being written off.

Nothing invented either

A line is proposed only where something in the record supports it, and it arrives with that evidence attached. An item with nothing behind it is never suggested, so review means confirming a short list rather than auditing a long one.

A stay that defends itself

The commonest inpatient rejection is a length of stay the notes do not justify. Here the bed days and the clinical entries supporting them come from the same source, so they cannot drift apart.

It is a draft, and it stays a draft. The claim is prepared for the responsible clinician or coder to review and sign. Nothing is submitted automatically. The saving is not in removing the human — it is in giving them a complete, evidenced list to approve instead of a blank form to reconstruct from memory at the end of the week.

1

The number that matters

Cost per patient encounter
A clinician seeing 20 patients a day over 22 days records about 440 encounters a month. At SAR 150 a seat, the software costs SAR 0.34 per encounter — and each of those encounters produces a note, the letters it generates, the diagnosis and procedure codes, a draft claim and the patient's own summary. That is the figure to hold against everything below.

Why it matters at scale

What an institution gets from this

Comprehensive documentation

Every encounter produces a full record rather than the shortest note that will do at the end of a long clinic. Completeness stops depending on how tired the clinician is.

Correct codes, first time

Codes are derived from the documentation and arrive with the line that supports them, so coding and record cannot drift apart.

Fewer denials

Most rejections turn on documentation that does not support the claim. When both come from one source, that gap closes before the claim is submitted.

Faster claims

Coding is ready when the note is, not days later after a separate review cycle — so submission is not waiting on a second pass.

Medicolegal defensibility

Contemporaneous, structured, and consistent across the note, the letters and the codes. The clinician reviews and signs off before anything is used.

Patients who understand

Every visit can produce a plain-language summary of what was found, what changed and what happens next — without anyone writing it twice.

Correspondence that keeps up

Referral and discharge letters are produced with the note rather than in a backlog, and go out as Word documents on your letterhead.

Departmental consistency

A department's own proformas become the house format for everyone in it, so records look the same whoever wrote them.

Six languages

Clinicians work in the language they think in; the patient summary can be produced in the language the patient reads.
